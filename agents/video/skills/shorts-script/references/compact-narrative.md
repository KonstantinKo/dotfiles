# Compact narrative playbook

A practical pathway for a Short built around a challenge, causal story, and satisfying
surprise. It draws on Jenny Hoyos's reported practice and the but/therefore storytelling
principle; see [sources](../sources.md). These techniques have successful precedents. The
numbers below are working defaults for this form, not guarantees or requirements for all Shorts.

**One-line method:** write the last line first, make an immediately compelling hook that
promises it, connect the middle through complications and consequences, and cut when the
complete payoff lands.

## Structure

| Part | Example timing for roughly 34 seconds | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0–3 s | Earn attention immediately, open a curiosity gap, promise its resolution. |
| Foreshadow | 3–7 s | Establish the obstacle or constraint and the finish line. |
| Narrative | 7–30 s | Develop 3–4 connected beats; each changes the situation. |
| Twist/payoff | Final 2–4 s | Fulfill the promise and add a surprising or emotionally rewarding detail. |

The final payoff sits inside the total runtime, so these approximate ranges can overlap.
Use 30–40 seconds as an initial sketch when the brief leaves duration open and the premise
fits that space. For another runtime, adjust the beats rather than mechanically stretching
these percentages. A longer version needs worthwhile developments, not a longer introduction.

## Hook: first frame and first words

The opening does work that a thumbnail does for a chosen video: it makes the appeal visible.

- Aim to establish the attraction in the first second; use the rest of the opening to make
  the promise clear. Show the result, disaster, odd object, or challenge immediately.
- Check the first frame on mute. Use a clear focal subject; a centered composition with no
  more than about three important objects is a useful simplification exercise.
- Try a 5–10-word spoken hook. Start with the premise, skipping greetings, “today I,” and
  background the viewer does not need yet.
- Put one unexpected element into a familiar setting. Prefer a concrete number, price, time,
  object, or named thing to an abstract claim.
- Words such as “banned,” “free,” “$1,” “secret,” “cheap,” “nobody,” and “only” can sharpen a
  truthful premise. Do not introduce unsupported claims or absolutes to use them.

Draft three hooks from different families and recommend one:

| Family | Starting move |
|---|---|
| Result first | Show an outcome that makes the viewer want to know how it happened. |
| Mistake | Make the failure or wrong assumption visible. |
| Constrained challenge | Set a goal with a budget, time limit, rival, or other obstacle. |
| One change | Show how one alteration changes an otherwise familiar situation. |
| Apparently impossible fact | Present a supported fact that conflicts with the viewer's expectation. |

Treat word and object counts as editing tools: if a slightly different opening is clearer,
keep it. The hook must still promise the video you can actually deliver.

## Foreshadow: make the finish line clear

Use one or two lines to establish an obstacle, a constraint, and what the ending will decide.
For example: “The shop's sandwich costs $6. I have $1 to make one that wins a blind taste test.”
This is an illustrative challenge, not a verified result or a verbatim creator quote.

For the 34-second sketch, test whether a new viewer can explain the challenge by around
seven seconds. Concrete stakes make the middle matter; they need not be dangerous or dramatic.

## Narrative: but / so

Outline 3–4 beats before writing full sentences. Connect them with “but” for a complication
and “so” or “therefore” for a response or consequence. Each beat should move the challenge
toward resolution while changing what the viewer expects.

An illustrative sequence: the budget rules out the usual ingredient → so try a substitute →
but it changes the texture → so change the preparation → test the result. Establish what
actually happened before presenting a factual experiment as a story.

Use “and then” as a diagnostic: if the only connection is chronology, inspect whether that
step creates a problem, changes the outcome, or earns its time. Replacing the conjunction
without changing the relationship fixes nothing. A genuinely procedural piece may be better
served by the demonstration pathway in SKILL.md.

## Payoff and twist

- Deliver the answer the hook promised, then the unexpected detail that makes it memorable:
  the aim is “satisfied, but surprised,” in Hoyos's formulation.
- Put the funniest line, biggest reveal, or warmest moment at the end. Let a necessary reaction
  finish the emotional payoff before cutting.
- Default to no separate outro, recap, or subscribe request in this compact cut. If the brief
  needs a CTA or useful synthesis, design its placement deliberately and assess the tradeoff.
- An optional loop can make the final line flow naturally into the first. It should still
  leave a first-time viewer satisfied, rather than conceal that the promised answer is missing.

Do not fabricate a twist. If the actual reward is an explanation, process, or straightforward
result, use the appropriate broader structure rather than making this one compulsory.

## Language, length, and visual rhythm

- Try approximately fifth-grade language for a broad audience: short sentences, familiar
  spoken words, and concrete descriptions. Preserve precision and the audience's useful
  specialist vocabulary when simplifying further would hurt the material.
- Make a compression pass aiming to cut roughly 30% from a loose first draft. Inspect phrases
  such as “basically,” “so what I did was,” and “you know.” This is an editing exercise, not a
  quota; retain factual uncertainty, necessary context, and characterful delivery.
- At 2.5–3 spoken words per second, 34 seconds permits roughly 85–100 words of continuous
  speech. Allow less when action, laughter, or reading needs time. Confirm with a read-through;
  the arithmetic is not a reason to rush a performer.
- Plan a meaningful visual development every few seconds: angle, text, B-roll, expression,
  or prop. Connect it to a beat. Keep a shot longer when anticipation, demonstration, or a
  reaction is doing useful work; decorative cutting is not a substitute for progression.

## Drafting workflow

1. Establish the audience, challenge, constraints, requested duration, and available visuals.
2. Write the payoff and final line first. If they are weak, sharpen the angle or choose another
   form before polishing the opening.
3. Write three hooks from different families; choose the one that best promises that ending.
4. Write the foreshadowing line and 3–4 but/so beats, then expand into spoken lines and shots.
5. Count words, read aloud with action and pauses, and make the compression pass.
6. Unless the user requests another format, deliver `Time | Beat | Line/audio | Shot`, then
   the three hook variants with a brief recommendation. Mark factual checks specifically.

## Review checklist for this pathway

Use pass / weak / fail for the applicable criteria. Lead with the biggest improvement, quote
the weak line or identify the missing shot, and propose a concrete revision. Judge whether a
departure from a numerical default harms the experience rather than failing it automatically.

1. The first frame makes the attraction legible on mute and shows the result, problem, or oddity.
2. The hook is compact, concrete, and opens the gap the video will close; 5–10 words is the target.
3. The setup quickly identifies the constraint and finish line; around seven seconds in the sketch.
4. The middle has connected complications and consequences rather than dispensable chronology.
5. Language is easy for the intended viewer to follow on a first listen, with little filler.
6. The spoken delivery and visual pauses fit the intended duration.
7. The ending fulfills the exact promise and adds the intended surprise or emotional reward.
8. The cut finishes the payoff without an unnecessary tail.
9. Any loop is natural and preserves a complete first viewing; omit this check if no loop is planned.
10. Visual changes support the beats, with enough time for the important moments to register.

## Read results and choose a revision

Use these as hypotheses for this structure, alongside the broader analytics guidance in
SKILL.md. Watch the corresponding scene before deciding what caused a retention pattern.

| Observed pattern | Inspect | A concrete revision to test |
|---|---|---|
| Low viewed versus swiped away | First-frame clarity, hook, and audience/topic fit | Show the compelling object/result sooner; sharpen the opening promise. |
| Drop around 5–8 seconds in a compact cut | Whether the setup stalls or leaves the finish line unclear | Compress setup and name what the ending will decide. |
| Steady loss through the middle | Repeated information, weak causal links, or unclear action | Remove a redundant step or make the real complication and response clearer. |
| Sharp dip at one moment | That line, shot, transition, or completed sub-payoff | Clarify or tighten the specific moment rather than automatically restructuring everything. |
| Departure in the final seconds | Whether the reward is finished and an outro remains | End after the complete reward if the remaining material adds nothing. |
| Retention over 100% | What viewers may be revisiting: enjoyment, detail, a loop, or confusion | Preserve an effective loop or clarify a difficult moment according to the evidence. |

Compare against similar videos on the channel over comparable observation windows. Repeat
the format with fresh substance, such as a new budget challenge, and compare hook families
across several Shorts. Avoid interpreting a single outlier or historical retention benchmark
as proof of what every future script needs.
