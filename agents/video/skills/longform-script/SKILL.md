# Long-form YouTube scripts (educational focus)

Three outcomes matter: people click (idea + packaging), stay (opening + structure), and learn
or accomplish something (starting knowledge + explanation + evidence). Choose the structure
and pace for the viewer's task.

**One-line model:** package an honest promise; establish a question the viewer cares about;
build from concrete evidence toward earned discoveries, with narration, visuals, and sources
planned together; land the insight or useful result.

Read [sources.md](sources.md) when checking attribution, evidence strength, or recommending
further study. It preserves the original references and adds the researched creator practices.

## 1. Idea and packaging come first
- The idea sets the ceiling. Top creators spend ~30% of their time on idea, title and
  thumbnail; small creators ~5% (Galloway's practitioner estimate, not a study).
- Draft the title and sketch the thumbnail **before** the full script. If it can't be
  packaged, reconsider the angle. For investigations, the question and promise are provisional;
  finalize the argument after reporting and revise the packaging if the evidence changes it.
- Title and thumbnail work as one unit: prefer complementary information over redundant text.
  The thumbnail passes the glance test (readable in a split second).
- "Legitbait", not "clicktraps" (Veritasium): irresistible packaging the video fully delivers.
  A vague title ("Strange Applications of the Magnus Effect") can sink a good video.
- Same effort, different pull: "50 Hours In Ketchup" beats "50 Hours In My Front Yard"
  (MrBeast memo). The transferable lesson is choosing an idea with a stronger image;
  spectacle is not a requirement for education.
- State the video's distinct contribution: a better explanation, original evidence, an unusual
  perspective, a useful test, or an actionable result. Brownlee rewards choosing his review
  with something distinctive without reversing the verdict merely to stand out.

## 2. Start from the viewer's actual starting point
Pin down assumed knowledge, the obstacle to understanding, and the destination: what viewers
will be able to explain, predict, decide, or do afterward. A misconception is one possible
obstacle; missing knowledge, an unanswered question, and a practical difficulty are others.
- When a relevant misconception exists, name it and confront it. Derek Muller's physics
  research found that polished explanations could leave mistaken beliefs unchanged, while
  misconception-based dialogue improved learning despite feeling more confusing. The original
  "nearly doubled learning" shorthand concerns particular study comparisons, not all videos.
- Let the viewer commit to a prediction, then show why it fails and build the correct model.
  "Most people think X" is useful only when X is a supported, relevant starting belief.
- Ben Eater imagined what his fourteen-year-old self would want to see and deliberately chose
  prerequisites. State assumptions instead of trying to explain every foundation.
- Sanderson distinguishes puzzles, which supply a shared start and endpoint, from exposition,
  which requires discovering what the audience knows and what new perspective to offer.

## 3. Concrete before abstract, question before answer
Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown):
- Start with a concrete example, puzzle or surprising pattern; build the general framework
  from examples rather than opening with an unmotivated abstraction.
- Put the key question up front so viewers try it before the answer.
- Aim for discovery: could the viewer almost have invented this? Visual first, name second.
  Motivate definitions by explaining the problem they solve and what alternatives fail.
- Mystery, tension and resolution can make an explanation a story. Genuine curiosity can
  motivate a pure puzzle; a forced practical application is not always necessary.
- Respect the audience; go deeper than surface description. Niche is fine.
- Give time to inspect diagrams, predict an outcome, or pause and solve a small problem.
  Consider a transfer question that uses the idea in a new situation. Viewing and feeling
  fluent are not equivalent to being able to use the knowledge.

## 4. Choose the structure and outline the payoffs

| Video type | Useful organizing structure |
|---|---|
| Conceptual explainer | Question → examples → model → application |
| Investigation or documentary | Question → evidence → competing explanations → conclusion |
| Practical tutorial | Desired result → prerequisites → steps → verification |
| Product review | User needs → testing → tradeoffs → recommendation |
| Advice video | Problem → options or points → examples → action |
| Build or experiment | Goal → attempts → obstacles → working result |

Ali Abdaal describes listicles, essays and stories as viable structures. Choose the form that
serves the task. Ben Eater's computer explanation became a component-by-component series;
complex topics may need several videos rather than one compressed account.

Use George Blackman's payoff-first approach: write each segment's discovery or result first,
then the question or problem that sets it up, then the evidence and explanation connecting them.
Every substantial segment should advance understanding or action, not merely postpone an answer.

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| Opening / first minute | Confirm the title/thumbnail's promise promptly; establish the question and why it matters. MrBeast calls this the most important minute; education may establish the promise without revealing the final answer. |
| Body | For causal stories, link beats by "but / therefore" rather than an unrelated "and then" list. For tutorials, show dependencies and steps; for reviews, meaningful comparisons. Resolve local questions and give a reason to continue at transitions. |
| Re-engagement | At sag points, use a meaningful new question, surprising result, or live demo. MrBeast plans one around minute 3; this is an entertainment heuristic, not a universal timer. Renew interest through progress and payoffs, allowing time for difficult ideas. |
| Ending | Land the insight, result, or recommendation and avoid a long outro. Add a brief recap, transfer check, action, or relevant next-video CTA when it helps the viewer; Abdaal recommends an end-screen next step. |

## 5. Write for eyes, ears, and evidence
Cleo Abram looks for the chart, diagram, archival material or demonstration that makes the
story work as video. Find the key visual during research; it can determine the angle.

| Time / beat | Ears: narration or interview | Eyes: visual action | Evidence / status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Estimated range | Spoken lines or intended sound bite | Specific diagram, demonstration, footage, document, or purposeful host appearance | Claim ID and source, calculation, test, or pending verification |

Plan words and visuals together. Generic stock footage may decorate a passage but does not
explain its mechanism. Use consistent visual representations to connect related ideas
(Sanderson); indicate what viewers should notice and give them time to see it.
A host's curiosity or reaction can guide the journey rather than merely fill a visual gap.

## 6. Research and verification
- Keep ambient research notes: questions, surprising connections and promising examples can
  become video ideas later (Sanderson). Stevens collects surprising findings and synthesizes
  their connections; preserve the creator's genuine intellectual curiosity.
- For a specific project, research questions before writing a conclusion. Collect useful
  visuals as well as facts. Keep the argument open to new evidence and testimony.
- Reviews: test the product and establish the judgment before scripting the verdict
  (Brownlee). Interviews and field experiences may need an outline rather than fixed dialogue.
- If reporting changes the conclusion, rewrite the introduction to fit the finished story
  (Abram). A simple entry promise can lead to a nuanced answer.
- Maintain a claim-level evidence record: claim ID, exact claim, source, finding / estimate /
  interpretation / prediction, conditions, disagreement, and verification status. Preserve
  qualifications such as "associated with" versus "causes". Never invent references.
- Following Kurzgesagt's process, check claims sentence by sentence, recalculate estimates,
  assess whether simplification misleads, and seek expert input when needed. For contentious
  topics, consider informed differing perspectives rather than only confirming sources.
- Check visual claims too: labels, scales, maps, rotation, timing, mechanisms, analogy limits,
  and distinctions between hypothetical and observed events. Recheck after storyboards and
  edits; accurate narration does not guarantee accurate animation.
- Keep unresolved facts visible. Mark a script ready only when material factual dependencies
  are verified; otherwise distinguish a structurally strong draft from a verified script.

## 7. Craft habits and revision
- Topic and explanation beat production polish; the early equipment investment worth making
  is a good microphone. Sanderson cautions against flashy animation that adds no understanding;
  simple slides or drawings can work.
- Clarity over over-explaining; research deeply; correct mistakes publicly (Tom Scott).
- Improvement compounds: ship regularly and iterate (Tom Scott's ten-year weekly streak;
  Sanderson: "just start"). Use a sustainable cadence suited to the format.
- Draft quickly before polishing (Abdaal's "Fast, Bad, Wrong"); separate rough draft,
  structural revision and final polish. His three-version limit is an optional antidote to
  perfectionism, not a ceiling on necessary factual fixes.
- Choose scripting depth: full narration for precise claims and animation timing; structured
  bullets for flexible expert delivery; questions and reporting goals for interviews.
  Blackman's hybrid writes setups and payoffs precisely, with bullets between them.
- Read aloud at the intended pace. Fix unspoken prose, unclear pronouns, unnecessary repetition,
  awkward sentences and visuals that need more time. Keep natural phrasing and actual beliefs;
  if AI helps organize material, review its wording and claims yourself.
- Prototype the explanation with a person near the target audience before costly production
  (Sanderson's sample lessons). Observe confusion, interest and what they can explain back;
  iterate the examples and sequence. If no audience test is available, state that limitation.

## Writing a script
1. Establish topic, viewer starting knowledge, obstacle, learning destination, distinct
   contribution, format, and target length. Label assumptions and ask only consequential gaps.
2. Draft three title + thumbnail concepts by default; state the chosen promise and key visual.
3. Research enough to support the angle. Write the final insight and intermediate payoffs;
   select a structure and outline how each segment earns its result and leads onward.
4. Draft an opening that confirms the promise and motivates the central question. Surface a
   relevant misconception or prediction when appropriate.
5. Develop concepts through concrete example → pattern → general statement as useful;
   plan narration, specific visuals and claim sources together. Mark meaningful re-engagement,
   thinking time, and approximate timings, not a fixed hook schedule.
6. Land the final insight or useful result; include only a purposeful brief next step.
7. Test the explanation when practical; revise structure, read aloud, verify claims and
   visual implications, and reconcile opening and packaging with the final evidence.
8. Output as appropriate to the stage: title/thumbnail options, timed beat outline, full
   script or deliberate hybrid, visuals and production needs, claim/source record, and the
   explicit list of facts still needing verification. Timings remain estimates until rehearsed;
   include diagram inspection, pauses and demonstrations instead of word count alone.

## Evaluating a script
Score each applicable item pass / weak / fail; mark inapplicable techniques N/A. Quote the line
or identify the missing beat, give a concrete rewrite, and lead with the biggest fix.
1. Strong, honest idea and packaging; the video delivers their promise.
2. Opening promptly establishes that promise, question and relevance.
3. Clear viewer starting point and destination; relevant misconceptions are confronted.
4. Motivated concepts, concrete examples and opportunities to think before answers.
5. Structure fits the task; causal beats connect by but/therefore where appropriate;
   segments deliver payoffs and transitions make continued attention worthwhile.
6. Meaningful progress and renewed interest at sag points, with enough processing time.
7. Each passage serves the core question or necessary prerequisite; repetition has a purpose.
8. Ending lands the insight or result; any recap or CTA earns its time.
9. Claims are accurate, qualified and checkable; verification gaps remain explicit.
10. Narration and visuals work together; visual implications and analogy limits are accurate.
11. Script is speakable, timing is plausible, and audience feedback has been addressed when available.

## Reading results (when analytics are given)
CTR informs packaging; average view duration and average percentage viewed inform attention.
They do not measure learning or usefulness directly. Compare with the channel's own averages,
videos of similar length, and comparable audiences / traffic sources.
- First-minute drop: investigate expectation mismatch, clarity, relevance and pace; slow promise
  delivery is one possible explanation, not a diagnosis.
- Mid-video dip: inspect whether viewers skip or abandon, and why. Cut or tighten when the
  passage adds little; preserve a necessary step and improve its explanation when needed.
- Spike: investigate valued / shared moments versus confusion requiring a replay. YouTube
  explicitly lists both; a spike alone does not mean "do more of this".
- For tutorials, departure after finding the answer can be successful service. Consider task
  completion, comments and ability to apply the idea alongside retention.
