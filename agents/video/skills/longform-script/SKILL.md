# Long-form YouTube scripts (educational focus)

Two things decide a long-form video: whether people click (idea + packaging) and whether they
stay (the first minute + structure). For education, a third: whether they actually learn,
which depends on confronting what they already believe.

**One-line model:** pick an idea you can package honestly and irresistibly; open by delivering
the thumbnail's promise and surfacing the viewer's wrong belief; build from concrete examples to
the general principle as a story with an open question at every transition.

Sources and evidence notes: `sources.md` in this folder.

## 1. Idea and packaging come first
- The idea sets the ceiling. Top creators spend ~30% of their time on idea, title and
  thumbnail; small creators ~5% (Galloway).
- Write the title and sketch the thumbnail **before** the script. If it can't be packaged,
  rework the idea, not the edit.
- Title and thumbnail are one unit: they complement, never repeat each other. The thumbnail
  passes the glance test (readable in a split second).
- "Legitbait", not "clicktraps" (Veritasium): irresistible packaging the video fully delivers.
  A vague title ("Strange Applications of the Magnus Effect") sinks a good video.
- Same effort, different pull: "50 Hours In Ketchup" beats "50 Hours In My Front Yard"
  (MrBeast memo). Choose the version of the idea with the stronger image.

## 2. Start from the viewer's misconception
Derek Muller's PhD research: clear, polished explanations left beliefs unchanged; videos that
stated the common misconception first and then dismantled it felt more confusing but nearly
doubled learning. Viewers arrive with wrong theories; a smooth explanation slides past them.
- Open with "most people think X", let the viewer commit (a prediction, a question), then show
  why X fails. Productive confusion is the point.

## 3. Concrete before abstract, question before answer
Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown):
- Start with a concrete example, puzzle or surprising pattern; build the general framework from
  examples, never the reverse.
- Put the key question up front so viewers try it before the answer.
- Aim for discovery: could the viewer almost have invented this? Visual first, name second.
- Tell it as a story: mystery, tension, resolution when it clicks.
- Respect the audience; go deeper than surface description. Niche is fine.

## 4. Retention structure

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| First minute | Deliver the thumbnail's promise immediately; state the question and why it matters. The most important minute (MrBeast memo). |
| Body | Sections linked by "but / therefore", never "and then"; each ends on an open question. |
| Re-engagement | At sag points (MrBeast plans one around minute 3), add a new mystery, surprising result or live demo. |
| Ending | Land the insight, then end fast. No long outro. |

## 5. Craft habits
- Topic beats production; the one early investment worth making is a good microphone.
- Clarity over over-explaining; research deeply; correct mistakes publicly (Tom Scott).
- Improvement compounds: ship regularly and iterate (Tom Scott's 10-year weekly streak;
  Sanderson: "just start").

## Writing a script
1. Pin down: topic, audience and prior knowledge, the misconception, target length.
2. Draft three title + thumbnail concepts; pick one and state its promise.
3. Write the opening: promise delivered, misconception surfaced, central question.
4. Outline sections as but/therefore beats; mark each section's open question and every
   re-engagement point.
5. For each concept: concrete example → pattern → general statement.
6. Write the ending: the insight in one line, then out.
7. Output: title/thumbnail options, beat outline with timestamps, full script, and a list of
   facts needing verification.

## Evaluating a script
Score each pass / weak / fail, quote the line, give a concrete rewrite. Lead with the biggest fix.
1. The idea and packaging are strong, honest, and the video delivers on them.
2. The first minute fulfils the thumbnail's promise.
3. The viewer's misconception is named and confronted, not skipped.
4. Concrete example before every abstraction; question before answer.
5. Sections connect by but/therefore; each ends on an open question.
6. Re-engagement at sag points; no stretch longer than a few minutes without a new hook.
7. Nothing is explained that the viewer doesn't need for the core question.
8. The ending lands the insight and stops.
9. Claims are accurate and checkable.

## Reading results (when analytics are given)
CTR grades the packaging; average view duration and average percentage viewed grade the
content. A steep drop in minute one means the promise wasn't delivered fast enough; a dip
mid-video marks a section to cut or tighten; spikes show what to do more of. Compare against
the channel's own averages.
