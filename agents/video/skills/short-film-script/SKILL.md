# Short film scripts

A short is a moment, not a life: one concept, one character, one situation, one turn. "Drama is
life with the dull bits cut out." The shorter the film, the less you build and the more you
reveal.

**One-line model:** find one idea you can say in a sentence; give it to one person who wants
something specific right now; make who, want and obstacle clear within a minute (within
seconds for Shorts); make the cost personal; show one small visible change; end at the turn.

Sources and evidence notes: `sources.md` in this folder.

Examples: Konstantin prefers heartfelt drama or gentle magic realism. Avoid horror and comedy
skits in example scripts unless asked.

## Concept
- Theme ≠ concept. "Grief" is a theme; "he has until midnight to save his dead mother's
  voicemail greeting" is a concept.
- Tests: one sentence without "and also"; one picture (would make a thumbnail); three ways it
  escalates; shootable with 1–2 locations and few actors.
- Concept-driven shorts: a rule or "what if" carries the film, escalation replaces arc (Lights
  Out: a figure that exists only in the dark). Character-driven shorts: a person under
  pressure carries it (most Omeleto picks). Know which carries yours.

## One character, one situation, one turn
- One protagonist; every other character is pressure (obstacle, temptation, mirror). If a
  character's removal costs the protagonist nothing, cut them.
- One place, one span of time. Avoid set pieces, many locations, time jumps (Atkinson).
- One turn: the reversal or reveal the rest sets up. If it needs subplots, it's a feature.

## First minute
- The audience knows what the film is about by the end of minute one (Film Fund): who, what
  they want now, what's in the way.
- Enter late: "as if it's already in progress." No alarm-clock openings. Arrive late, leave
  early in every scene.
- Characterize through a choice under small pressure, not dialogue.

## Stakes
Omeleto looks for "meaningful stakes for a strongly defined character", plausible obstacles,
and an arc. Raise stakes without budget:
1. Personal, not global.  2. A clock.  3. Irreversible.
4. An inner stake under the outer one.  5. Close the exits: the obvious fix fails on screen.
Obstacles grow out of the situation; scenes connect by "but / therefore", never "and then".

## Arc
- A small, visible change, not a transformation (Atkinson).
- Want (outer goal) vs need (inner truth). The turn is the character giving up the want for
  the need, or getting the want and finding it doesn't matter.
- Mirror moment: the same action at start and end, done differently.

## Ending
- End at or right after the climax (Film Fund).
- A twist must re-read what came before, not just surprise. Plant it early.
- Meaning may be open; what happened must be clear to a general YouTube audience.
- End on an image or a reaction, not an explanation.

## Micro-stories for YouTube Shorts (30 s – 3 min)
Shorts can be up to 3 minutes (since 15 Oct 2024), vertical or square. The Shorts rules
(`shorts-script`) apply on top: the first frame is the thumbnail, works on mute, hard cut at
the end.

| Length | Script | Shots | Beats | Fits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15–30 s | ⅓ page | 5–10 | 2–3 | Gag, magic fix, single reversal |
| 45–60 s | ≈1 page | 15–20 | 4–5 | Reversal or mirror micro-arc |
| 90 s | 1½ pages | 25–35 | 5–6 | Escalation or arc with one complication |
| 2–3 min | 2–3 pages | 35–60 | 6–8 | Compressed Story Spine |

- Flip stories (≤60 s) change the viewer's understanding; arc stories (60 s+) change the
  character. Under 60 s, don't force an arc.
- Use situations everyone already knows (first date, bike lesson, group chat): setup costs
  zero seconds.
- One piece of information per shot; ~15–20 shots per minute. Needing more usually means
  overthinking.
- Carry exposition with objects, phone screens, on-screen text, off-screen dialogue.
- Six-word test: if the story can't be told in six words, it isn't clear yet.

Micro-structures: **Reversal** (expectation → commitment → flip), **Magic Fix** (everyday
problem → impossible visual fix → twist; Zach King), **Escalation** (goal + clock → three
growing obstacles → turn), **Mirror** (action A → pressure → choice → action A′),
**Compressed Story Spine** (Pixar; Harmon's circle merged into four beats), **Loop** (last
shot leads back into the first).

## From vague concept to script
1. Make it specific: one person, one place, one device or object.
2. List five frictions; pick the visible one with feeling underneath.
3. Decide the last image first; work backward.
4. One line each: want, need, clock.
5. Six words, then logline: "When [character] [incident], they must [want] before [clock],
   but [obstacle]."
6. Pick a structure; fill the Story Spine ("Once upon a time… Every day… One day… Because of
   that… Because of that… Until finally…"), each line caused by the last.
7. Beat sheet with a seconds budget; cut beats, not words.
8. Shot list; mark the first frame and final image.
9. Write action first, add dialogue only where a gesture can't do it, then halve the
   dialogue. Write sounds into the script. Read aloud with a timer. 1 page ≈ 1 minute.

When stuck: who does this matter to most; what do they do alone; what object sums them up;
what do they want tonight; what stops them visibly; the worst moment for this; what they'd
never do; who else makes it harder; the opposite of the obvious ending; the last image without
dialogue.

## Writing a script
Clarify genre, target length, platform (Shorts vertical vs horizontal), cast and locations.
Then output: six-word story, logline, chosen structure with reason, beat sheet with timings,
shot list for micro-lengths, and formatted pages. Flag anything that exceeds the length budget.

## Evaluating a script
Score each pass / weak / fail, quote the line, give a concrete rewrite. Lead with the biggest fix.
1. Concept in one sentence and one picture; concept- vs character-driven is clear.
2. One protagonist; supporting characters apply pressure.
3. One situation; locations and time span fit the length.
4. Starts in progress; who / want / obstacle clear on time for the length.
5. Stakes personal, irreversible, on a clock; obvious fixes fail on screen.
6. Scenes connect by but/therefore.
7. One clear turn, planted earlier.
8. Change shown through action (mirror), not stated.
9. Ends at the climax on an image; what happened is clear.
10. Dialogue lean; sound written in; length matches the page and shot budget.
